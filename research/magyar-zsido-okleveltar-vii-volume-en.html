FOREWORD

CONTRIBUTIONS TO THE SETTLEMENT HISTORY OF HUNGARIAN JEWRY
IN THE FIRST HALF OF THE 18TH CENTURY

The settlement history of the Jews of Hungary is still waiting to be written. The present publication provides source material for carrying out this work. The earlier volumes of our Oklevéltár [charter collection] do contain numerous data on the immigration of the Jews into the Hungarian homeland, their settlement here and the formation of their communities; however, it is in the material published here that we receive, for the first time, official data on their enumeration extending to the whole country and carried out county by county. With this we become acquainted with the largest and most enduring — by now the fourth — settlement of Jewry in the country encircled by the Carpathians.

Surveying the entire process of the settlement history of Jewry on Hungarian soil, starting from the earliest times, we can distinguish four periods*. In Roman Pannonia, along the Danubian limes, in the 3rd and 4th centuries, the surviving inscribed and symbolic monuments point to the presence of Syrian and Asia Minor Jews. These settlements, small in number, were unable to preserve their continuity; they disappear in the centuries of the Migration Period. Only recently discovered inscribed stone monuments proclaim their former existence.

In the Hungarian Middle Ages, from the 12th century, we can follow the traces of renewed Jewish immigration; royal protection made it possible for those who had immigrated from southern Germany to settle in the emerging towns. The expulsion decree of King Louis the Great [Nagy Lajos], stemming from religious intolerance, represents only a brief interruption of a few years in the medieval settlement history of the Jews of Hungary. The disaster of Mohács and the turmoil and internal wars that followed it led to the rapid destruction of the urban Jewish congregations that had by then existed for centuries (Buda, Esztergom, Székesfehérvár, Sopron, Pozsony, Nagyszombat.).

The third is the period of immigration into the territory of Hungary under Turkish rule and of the Principality of Transylvania. The victories of the war of liberation (1683-169D [OCR: final digit garbled]) and the fall of the independent Transylvanian principality close this era.

The fourth immigration process began when, after the lengthy Turkish wars, the century-long national struggles and then the devastating epidemics, the country passed into the possession of the Habsburgs largely depopulated. A veritable new conquest [honfoglalás] became necessary to repopulate the country and develop it economically. In this a task awaited the relatively small Jewish population as well. The mercantilist ideas of the age were by then already spreading, and the examples of certain Western states had their effect, when the aristocratic great landowners gave Jews the opportunity to settle and to take part in economic life. The aristocratic leaders of the country did not follow the reconstruction plan worked out under the influence of Archbishop Kollonich (Einrichtungs Werkh des Königreiche Hungaren, H(89) [OCR: year garbled]), in which he recommends that the Jews living in fairly considerable numbers in Hungary be gradually removed from the country and that where they are not yet present — thus especially in the newly acquired territories — they should not be admitted at all.

As is well known, the great settlement campaigns after the wars of liberation ran in two directions. On the one hand, a movement began from the more densely populated border regions of the country, where the raging wars had not reduced the serf population to such a great extent, toward the interior of the country. Here the great landowners engaged in settlement offered favourable living conditions to the immigrants. On the other hand, settlers were invited from neighbouring countries to the abandoned territories.

We can observe the same duality in the course of the settlement history of the Jews of Hungary in this period. Here too we find that their movement into the counties of the interior begins from the western, north-western and then north-eastern border regions, which were by then more densely inhabited by Jews; on the other hand, at the same time we know of smaller and larger seigneurial settlement schemes, primarily from neighbouring Moravia and Bohemia, as well as of the settling of individual families from Polish territory into the villages of the north-eastern border region.

It could be the subject of a separate study to show how it became possible that the first official censuses, already in the 1720s, record a relatively large Jewish population in the border counties of Transdanubia and the North-western Uplands.
