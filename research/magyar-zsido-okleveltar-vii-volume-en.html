FOREWORD

CONTRIBUTIONS TO THE SETTLEMENT HISTORY OF HUNGARIAN JEWRY
IN THE FIRST HALF OF THE 18TH CENTURY

The settlement history of the Jews of Hungary is still waiting to be written. The present publication provides source material for carrying out this work. The earlier volumes of our Oklevéltár [charter collection] do contain numerous data on the immigration of the Jews into the Hungarian homeland, their settlement here and the formation of their communities; however, it is in the material published here that we receive, for the first time, official data on their enumeration extending to the whole country and carried out county by county. With this we become acquainted with the largest and most enduring — by now the fourth — settlement of Jewry in the country encircled by the Carpathians.

Surveying the entire process of the settlement history of Jewry on Hungarian soil, starting from the earliest times, we can distinguish four periods*. In Roman Pannonia, along the Danubian limes, in the 3rd and 4th centuries, the surviving inscribed and symbolic monuments point to the presence of Syrian and Asia Minor Jews. These settlements, small in number, were unable to preserve their continuity; they disappear in the centuries of the Migration Period. Only recently discovered inscribed stone monuments proclaim their former existence.

In the Hungarian Middle Ages, from the 12th century, we can follow the traces of renewed Jewish immigration; royal protection made it possible for those who had immigrated from southern Germany to settle in the emerging towns. The expulsion decree of King Louis the Great [Nagy Lajos], stemming from religious intolerance, represents only a brief interruption of a few years in the medieval settlement history of the Jews of Hungary. The disaster of Mohács and the turmoil and internal wars that followed it led to the rapid destruction of the urban Jewish congregations that had by then existed for centuries (Buda, Esztergom, Székesfehérvár, Sopron, Pozsony, Nagyszombat.).

The third is the period of immigration into the territory of Hungary under Turkish rule and of the Principality of Transylvania. The victories of the war of liberation (1683-169D [OCR: final digit garbled]) and the fall of the independent Transylvanian principality close this era.

The fourth immigration process began when, after the lengthy Turkish wars, the century-long national struggles and then the devastating epidemics, the country passed into the possession of the Habsburgs largely depopulated. A veritable new conquest [honfoglalás] became necessary to repopulate the country and develop it economically. In this a task awaited the relatively small Jewish population as well. The mercantilist ideas of the age were by then already spreading, and the examples of certain Western states had their effect, when the aristocratic great landowners gave Jews the opportunity to settle and to take part in economic life. The aristocratic leaders of the country did not follow the reconstruction plan worked out under the influence of Archbishop Kollonich (Einrichtungs Werkh des Königreiche Hungaren, H(89) [OCR: year garbled]), in which he recommends that the Jews living in fairly considerable numbers in Hungary be gradually removed from the country and that where they are not yet present — thus especially in the newly acquired territories — they should not be admitted at all.

As is well known, the great settlement campaigns after the wars of liberation ran in two directions. On the one hand, a movement began from the more densely populated border regions of the country, where the raging wars had not reduced the serf population to such a great extent, toward the interior of the country. Here the great landowners engaged in settlement offered favourable living conditions to the immigrants. On the other hand, settlers were invited from neighbouring countries to the abandoned territories.

We can observe the same duality in the course of the settlement history of the Jews of Hungary in this period. Here too we find that their movement into the counties of the interior begins from the western, north-western and then north-eastern border regions, which were by then more densely inhabited by Jews; on the other hand, at the same time we know of smaller and larger seigneurial settlement schemes, primarily from neighbouring Moravia and Bohemia, as well as of the settling of individual families from Polish territory into the villages of the north-eastern border region.

It could be the subject of a separate study to show how it became possible that the first official censuses, already in the 1720s, record a relatively large Jewish population in the border counties of Transdanubia and the North-western Uplands.

From the standpoint of settlement history, the question naturally arises: from where and when did this relatively large Jewish population move into the country? The assumption cannot be accepted that a “sizeable” Jewry already lived here in Turkish times, as a remnant of the descendants of those driven out of the medieval towns. We insert here the oldest Kismarton Jewish census known so far, from 1564, although this Jewish congregation can boast the greatest continuity of Jewish population. Of the eight permanent residents, two were born in Kismarton, three in Sopron, two in Kőszeg, and one was of Moravian origin. (M. Zs. O. These are scant remnants of the medieval Jewry of Sopron, Kőszeg and Kismarton. With reference to these, the legislature (1578) could truly write: “Judaei ... , qui in Hungaria paucissimi sunt, quo citius cmigrent; ad solvendum dicam ct alia onera subeunda, in duplo cogantur” [Latin: “The Jews ..., who are very few in Hungary, shall be compelled to pay the tax and bear other burdens double, so that they may emigrate the sooner”]. The law thus even urges the emigration of these few by means of double taxation. Yet these insignificant congregations formed the seeds of the more populous Jewish communities that we encounter here in the first half of the 18th century. In investigating their origins, we may think primarily of immigration from Lower Austria, where, besides the Jewish quarter that developed in the city of Vienna, a whole series of rural Jewish settlements arose in the course of the 17th century*. These were admittedly not yet very populous; even the largest numbered no more than 25–45 families. Similar to these were the Sopron county communities of this period, which are mentioned in the documents (Kismarton 1623, Nagymarton 1(39) [OCR; probably 1639]). The immigration of Jews naturally always depended on the authorities or landlords receiving them, who permitted their residence there and their permanent settlement, and who could provide the newcomers with the necessary protection and security. This must always be taken into account when describing the settlement history of Jewry.
The Jewry expelled at the end of the Middle Ages from the towns of the western border strip was given temporary refuge by the lords of the neighbouring castle estates (Baron Weiszpriach, lord of the castles of Kismarton and Nagymarton; likewise Ecchius von Salm, castellan of Pozsony; Jurisich Miklós, commander of the castle of Kőszeg, who, however, in 1540 yielded to the request of the town burghers and expelled the Jews from there, although they had honourably held their ground in the defence of Kőszeg castle in 1532.)
The Jews, accustomed to the conditions of medieval urban life, were forced in their new places of residence to adapt to the changed circumstances of life. Many of them, who showed no inclination to do so, sought a suitable livelihood elsewhere, far abroad. It is characteristic of those who remained in the villages that in the early period they mostly took up dealing in livestock and animal products. Greater security and wider economic opportunities came to the Jewish inhabitants of these communities when the places became centres of the estates of great landowning feudal lords (the Esterházys, Batthyánys, Zichys, Pálffys). The Jews of these communities paid their lords a fixed annual sum in gratitude for their protection and defence (Schutzgeld), but beyond this they also strove in every possible way to make themselves useful, performing the most varied services. They sought their lords’ favour by every means, for the security of their existence depended entirely on them; this dependent social position made them servile. On commission they carried out the most varied purchases for their lords and ladies, which were needed in the Baroque era to maintain their luxurious courts. They marketed the surplus produce of the landlords, who by then had largely switched to demesne (manorial) farming. They undertook the lease of seigneurial rights, the regalia; they leased the butcher’s shop, the mill, brandy distilling and beer brewing, as well as the retail sale of drinks. Even if these did not always yield a large income, the constant connection with the lords of the estate meant a great deal to the Jewish settlers, because it ensured some protection for the newcomer, who often lived alone. They also leased the collection of seigneurial tolls and ferry dues, which was no easy task and made them unpopular among the population. Thus this often led to clashes at the designated toll stations. They became instruments of seigneurial exploitation, and although the law from 1630 onward [OCR “Ót11”] repeatedly forbade Jews from leasing the thirtieths and tolls (“.Judaei tam a tricesimarum, quam teloniorum arendatione amoveantnr” [Latin: “Jews shall be removed from leasing both the thirtieths and the tolls”], ]630 [OCR; = 1630]. The freedom struggle of Rákóczi Ferenc was a great ordeal for the by then numerous Jewish inhabitants of the western border region, since in their places of residence they were all in the service of royalist magnates. The kuruc attacks were directed against their communities as well; generalizing, the kuruc proclaimed that the Jews all sided with the Germans. When the struggles of the Rákóczi freedom war came to an end and the devastating epidemics that followed it ceased, the Jewish communities were repopulated, the refugees returned, and indeed, in the ensuing period of peace, a larger process of immigration began from Moravia and Bohemia. Data on Jewish settlement conditions in this period are provided by the Jewish censuses carried out in the twenties of the century, which the Hungarian Royal Lieutenancy Council, newly organized at that time, ordered county by county on royal command. The royal decree lS [OCR; probably “és” = “and”] the Lieutenancy Council’s instruction inquired into the numbers, occupations and tax conditions of Hungarian Jewry because it wished to prevent their renewed mass immigration and indeed wished to reduce the number of those who had been resident for some time. The counties submitted the data in varying ways. In each county the censuses were carried out by the district magistrates (szolgabírák) and jurors, who after completing their work presented their reports to the county general assembly, which sent the material collected for the county to the Lieutenancy Council. The Jews, who lived on the estates of the lords of the noble counties and stood in their service, brought them profit — this explains why the nobility did not wish or demand their removal, and indeed made the arrival of new ones possible.

In this period newcomers arrive in greatest numbers from two areas: Moravia, Bohemia and Poland.

Bp., 1896.) showed, on the basis of the data of the 1735–38 census, that more than one third of the Jewry of Hungary at that time declared itself to be of Moravian origin (38,:35% [OCR; probably 38,35%])*. The censuses from the twenties of the 18th century published in this volume confirm this finding. At the same time, however, we must state that this large-scale immigration of Moravian Jews cannot be attributed to King Charles III’s decree of 1726, valid for Bohemia and Moravia, by which he restricted the number of Jews in these two provinces of his empire, stipulating that in each family only one marrying son should receive the right of residence (incolatus). It was then considered whether to extend the closed number to our country as well, so as to restrict Jewish families; however, taking into account the demographic and economic condition of the country, which had then recently been liberated and was still sparsely populated, the chancellery did not recommend extending this decree to Hungary — adding that its introduction would “veluti piures malas sequelas post se trahere” [Latin: “as it were, bring several bad consequences in its train”]. The lords of the country at that time — as can be established — willingly received onto their estates Jews who possessed some property, because they could profit from them economically.
Immigration from Moravia, just like that from Austria and — as we shall see — that from Polish land, demonstrably (as the documents show) began at the start of the 17th century and continued steadily in the following century too, whereas no new immigrants could come from Austrian soil from the last quarter of the 17th century, after they had been expelled from there in lü70-71 [OCR; probably 1670–71]. By contrast, new groups of immigrants continuously arrived from Moravian soil, since the Jewry there showed considerable numerical growth in this period.

Of the historical past of Moravian Jewry we know the following: until the middle of the 15th century, the towns of this Slav-inhabited territory, which were then taking shape, served as the main stations on the eastward route of the Jewry expelled from the imperial cities of Germany, and in them the Jews fulfilled a useful economic function. From the second half of the 15th century, however, the Jews were expelled from the royal towns of the province — with the consent of the ruler (King László V) and under the influence of the anti-Jewish agitation of Kapisztrán János [John of Capistrano]. These towns (Brünn, Olmütz, Znaim, Iglau etc.) obtained from the ruler the right not to be obliged to tolerate Jews within their walls (“de non tolerando judaeos”). Here too it happened, as in many places in the German Empire, that the Jewry pushed out of the towns was partly received by the landlords of neighbouring communities. In this way more and more rural Jewish congregations formed, whose number in time exceeded fifty. The number of Jewish inhabitants of these communities grew when, in the middle of the 17th century, from 16M~ [OCR; year unclear] onward, following the Cossack attacks, the massacres began on Ukrainian, Polish and Lithuanian soil that brought about the break-up of these densely Jewish-inhabited areas. Eastern Jewry — on its route of flight westward — sought protection and the possibility of settlement in the nearest Moravian communities. The number of inhabitants of the Moravian Jewish communities was likewise increased by the masses expelled from Vienna and Lower Austria, and indeed — as is known — Jewish captives carried off from Buda (1686) and Belgrade (Hi89 [OCR; year unclear]), recaptured from the Turks, were transported to Moravia, because the high ransom demanded for them was hoped for from the Jews there.
From a whole series of documents we learn that, from the beginning of the 17th century, Jews had a significant share in the border trade between Hungary and Moravia. The Hungarian landlords early on took into their service Moravian Jews experienced in commercial matters and gave them the opportunity to settle on their estates. As early as 1599 the Czobor estate had a Jewish toll collector. Conspicuous is the relatively small number of Jews in the north-eastern region, although this territory neighboured Poland, where since the end of the 15th century European Jewry, squeezed out of the central territories of Europe, had concentrated and accumulated in numbers of several hundred thousand. We must also take into account that from this populous Jewish centre a large-scale emigration westward began at the time of the Cossack attack (1648–1 65ö [OCR; probably 1656]). The question naturally arises why the flood of refugees did not turn toward the neighbouring territories beyond the Carpathians. There were certainly historical and economic reasons for this. We know from the history of Jewish migrations that when our forebears were compelled to leave their homes, they mostly sought refuge with their Jewish brethren. Jews were then hardly to be found among the sparsely populated, poor Ruthenians of Subcarpathia. Right up to the beginning of the 17th century we know of no Jews living here. The Jews of Poland begin to appear in the first half of the 17th century at vintage time in the Hegyalja region, to buy grapes there and carry home the wine made from them. The 13 north-eastern counties of Hungary belonged economically under the Szepes Chamber.                         7. The counties of the separating zone

     Here we must point out that the three counties last listed formed part of that zone in which Jews either did not live or were not permitted to live. The counties of this zone to some extent separated the western and eastern immigrant Jewish groups from each other. This separating area begins in the north in the Szepesség, where only in Hunfalva could some Jews live. It continued across the territory of Gömör county, which as late as 1752 reports to the Lieutenancy Council that no Jew has lived on its territory for many years, and which in its statute drawn up in 17U7 [OCR; year unclear] declared: “years ago, for well-founded reasons, the universitas of the county resolved that no nobleman may admit a Christ-denying Jew harmful to the common good.” Of our censuses, only that of 1746 mentions five Jewish families in the county: two in Putnok and three in three villages close to it, 24 souls in all. The zone continues with Torna county, where only one family resided in 1728, then with Heves and Külső Szolnok counties, where at the time of the 173ü [OCR; possibly 1736] census 8 families did live in Gyöngyös and one in Gyöngyös Halászi, but in the following year the two united counties expel the Jews: “solely for the purpose of protecting the inhabitants of the county”. In the privileged territories, in the Nagy Kunság (Greater Cumania) and in the towns of the Hajdúság, Jews could not live. In response to the Lieutenancy Council's call for a census of the Jews, the administrator of the Jász-Kun district writes from Jászberény in 1745 that not a single Jew is to be found in their district; only from time to time does one or another obtain permission to carry on trade in petty goods, travelling hither and thither. The captain of the Hajdú towns reports in 1744, when the census of Jews is requested of him — referring to his earlier replies: “In our towns not even one Jew has yet been found, so now too we can write nothing other than that.” Békés county, as we have seen, did not admit Jews. The one or two families mentioned in the 1735 and 1746 censuses form an exception. In Csanád too only one landlord admitted a few persons. Csongrád county reports on the occasion of the 174G [OCR; possibly 1746] census that there is not a single Jew in the county holding a residence permit. The existence of this separating zone over several decades in some way restricted the mass advance of eastern Jewry towards the western parts of our country, and in this way separated the eastern and western Jewry, which differed somewhat from each other in customs, culture and language. This found expression in the distinction, long current within Hungarian Jewry, which designated the western Jewry as “oberlandi” and the eastern as “unterlandi”.

10. The Jewry of the Fortresses and Towns

Having reviewed the conscriptions of the Jewry of the counties, we must also discuss the conscriptions made in the fortresses and towns of the country.
Of the fortresses, Győr, Komárom, Esztergom, Buda, Nagyvárad, Arad and Pétervárad are relevant here. The beginnings of the Jewry of these fortresses can be traced back partly to the time of the sixteen-year war of liberation, and then to the Rákóczi War of Independence and the Turkish wars of the first half of the 18th century. At this time the Jewish army contractors living in Vienna, Oppenheimer Sámuel, Wertheirner [OCR; probably Wertheimer] Simson, Lehmann Herz and the others, played an important role in equipping and provisioning the troops stationed in the fortresses. Much information about them can be found in the work of Jrfax Grumoald [OCR garbled; probably Max Grunwald]: Samuel Oppenheimer und sein Kreis (Wien-Leipzig, ] 913. [OCR; probably 1913]). The first Jews came to the fortresses as agents of these Vienna-based contractors, enjoying the protection of the chamber administrators and fortress captains. They obtained the right of settlement in the fortresses or in the Váralja [area below the castle; OCR: 'il. V áralja']. Once the wartime had passed, the chamber and military administration confined itself to a narrower sphere. The towns that had grown up around the fortresses, and the counties, then sought to extend their jurisdiction to the Jews living there. As a result, conflicts arose between the chamber and military authorities and the civilian population.
We see this in Győr, where the 1725 conscription finds Hirschl Izsák with his whole family under the protection of the fortress commander. He later became keeper of the military depots of Győr, Székesfehérvár and Veszprém. In one of his petitions, dated 1725, he signs himself: "Isaac Hirschl Judaeus jaurinensis caesareus".                                         INTRODUCTION


               THE NATIONWIDE CENSUSES OF JEWS IN HUNGARY
                     IN THE FIRST HALF OF THE 18TH CENTURY

      Among the most valuable sources for the history of Hungarian Jewry in the first half of the 18th century are the documents, surviving in various forms, that contain the results of the nationwide Jewish censuses held at that time; these documents themselves we are accustomed to call censuses. The special source value of these censuses lies in the fact that, at certain points in time when the Jews were enumerated, they illuminate the economic and social, and likewise the legal, situation of the entire Jewry living in the territory of Hungary of that time — in the narrower sense. From them we learn, among other things, where Jews lived, how many there were, what they occupied themselves with, and who paid what, to whom, and on what title. Without knowledge of these data it would not be possible to write the history of Jewry in a proper manner.
      We have no knowledge of any Jewish census of nationwide character in Hungary, i.e. one extending to the entire territory of the country, before 1725. We do know, however, of a few Jewish censuses prepared for individual regions of so-called Upper Hungary, which belonged to a separate treasury administration, that is, for one part of the country. These censuses are known from 1698 and 1699; they were prepared, on the order of the treasury authority of Upper Hungary, the Szepes Chamber, by the thirtieth-duty (harmincad) offices subordinate to the Chamber, and accordingly they contain the data on the enumerated Jewry arranged by thirtieth-duty districts. From 1698 we know of the Jewish censuses of five Upper Hungarian thirtieth-duty districts, namely those of Bártfa, Eperjes, Lubló, Szatmár and Sztropkó. From 1699 we know of the Jewish censuses of six Upper Hungarian thirtieth-duty districts, namely those of Bártfa, Homonna, Kurima, Szatmár, Szőllős and Sztropkó. They received decrees from it and sent their submissions to it. It did occur, however, that they petitioned the ruler directly in some matter.
     The Lieutenancy Council, organized in 1724, was a national government authority. The administration of the country fell within its competence, except for chamber or treasury matters and military matters. For the former, the national government authority was the Hungarian Royal Chamber (camera regia Hungarica); the latter were handled until 1740

       In connection with the role of the Lieutenancy Council, mention must also be made of the national or provincial commissariat (commissariatus provincialis), which took part in the Jewish census of 1746-1748. This commissariat operated as an authority subordinate to the Lieutenancy Council; its competence covered military matters affecting the civilian population and, in connection with these, the collection and accounting of the state tax. The director (director) of the commissariat was one of the councillors of the Lieutenancy Council. Subordinate to him, and through him to the Lieutenancy Council, were the national or provincial district commissioners or chief commissioners (commissarii, supremi commissarii). In the 1740s Hungary in the narrower sense had 7 national or provincial commissariat districts (districtus): those of Pozsony, Besztercebánya, Sopron, Buda, Kassa, Debrecen and Eszék. They operated in parallel and in harmony with the war commissariats; through them the Lieutenancy Council was able to call on the support of the military and, when necessary, to enforce its decrees by armed force against the counties, towns and districts.
       Legally the Lieutenancy Council was directly subordinate to the ruler; it addressed its submissions to him, and the royal dispositions arriving at the Lieutenancy Council spoke in his name. In reality, however, these royal dispositions were prepared by the Hungarian Royal Court Chancellery (cancellaria regia Hungarica aulica); the Lieutenancy Council's submissions came in to it, and it laid them before the ruler accompanied by its own proposals. In practice the Chancellery was the superior authority of the Lieutenancy Council.
       The royal dispositions concerning Jewish affairs, including the matter of the censuses, in the period between 1725 and 1748 were all prepared at the Hungarian Royal Court Chancellery.
       We would be mistaken, however, if we believed that the series of administrative organs that dealt with Hungarian affairs — and thus with the affairs of Jewry and with the matter of the Jewish censuses — ended with the Chancellery, and that after the Chancellery came the ruler himself. Legally this was indeed the situation. Here too, however, reality differed from the legal situation. The Hungarian king was at the same time the ruler of the Habsburg empire. Besides the Hungarian government authorities there were also imperial government organs. These he consulted — contrary to Hungarian laws — in Hungarian matters as well. In Hungarian matters the ruler's decisions were more than once born in these imperial government organs, foreign from the Hungarian point of view. This finding applies also to Jewish affairs and to the matter of the Jewish censuses.
       In what follows, when we speak of royal decrees, we must be aware that the roots of these decrees may be sought, besides the Lieutenancy Council and the Chancellery, in these imperial government organs as well — naturally without leaving the rulers themselves out of consideration. From that part of the decree's introduction which refers to measures taken concerning Jewry in other provinces of the empire, we may infer that the initiator was one of the imperial government organs. It can be regarded as almost certain that the initiative did not come from the Hungarian government authorities.
      The decree starts from the premise that Jewry, if its number exceeds a certain measure, is to the detriment of the population [OCR: "bkosság kárára YI1I1"]. On the basis of this consideration, certain provinces of the empire, the so-called hereditary provinces, decided to reduce the number of Jews living in their territory. For Hungary these measures meant the danger that the Jews expelled from the hereditary provinces would come here. The ruler therefore calls upon the Lieutenancy Council to prevent the immigration of Jews and, further, to reduce the number of those already present, or to submit its proposal concerning the achievement of these aims.
      In connection with the above, the ruler also orders the Lieutenancy Council to have the entire Jewry living in the country enumerated. As to what the census should be like, the royal decree says little: it should show where Jews live and what public burdens they bear.⁸
       The Lieutenancy Council forwarded the royal command to the counties, towns and districts in its circular decree dated 9 July 1725 [OCR: "júl. H-én"; day read from note 9]. It instructed them, on the one hand, to prevent by appropriate measures foreign Jews from moving into their territory, and on the other hand, to enumerate the Jews living in their territory.
