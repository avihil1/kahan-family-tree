Family history: Kahan in Gyulafalu (Giulești)

Research notes by D. Shezaf, originally published on WikiTree (last modified 29 Sep 2024).

The tl;dr: Chaim Arye Leb Kahana (1903-1956), born in a village in Máramaros (Maramureș) county in Romania, was a rabbi in Timișoara, Romania and then in Tel-Aviv, Israel. Among his descendants several have public presence in Israel, including rabbi Meir Kahana. The genealogical research shows that despite being Kahana from Máramaros, they are not immediately related to the Kahana-Hellers from Sziget (Sighetu). Instead the roots of the family are likely in Moldova. Interestingly, people in the family used the two surnames Moldovan and Kahana interchangeably as late the the 1880's.

I got interested in the Kahan family of Gyulafalu because the Hebrew Wikipedia implies it is related to the Kahana-Heller "clan" that played a central role in the Jewish community of Marmores Sziget from the 18th to the 20th century. The Kahana-Hellers have been extensively researched and thousands of descendents are known, but I found no documentation for how the Gyulafalu branch is related. So I thought it would be fun to try and find out.

However the research took me down a different path, and I now believe the Gyulafalu Kahans are not in fact closely related to the Kahana-Hellers, but instead they are a different Cohanim family, originating in Moldova.

Maramaros (Hungarian; Romanian Maramures) is a historic county of Transylvania, in the Carptian mountains. Between 1711 and 1918 it was part of the Kingdom of Hungary inside the Habsburg empire. The county seat is Sziget (Sighetu), a town of 11000 people in 1880 and 27000 in 1940.

Gyulafalu (Giulești) - also known in Hungarian as Gyulafalva, Maragyulafalu or Maragyulafalva - is one of a group[ of small villages in the mountainous south of the county, about 25km from Sziget. A writer who lived in the area in the 1990s commented "I had thought I had been born too late to see anything like the peasant life about which Tolstoy and Hardy had written, but I was wrong. Here there was a remnant of an old, almost medieval world, cut off by the mountains and forests." (Quoted in this essay)

The first mention of a Kahan in the records in Gyulafalu is in 1856, when Leb Kahan was a Sandak for a new baby boy. Two years later, he has a daughter named Dwora. He goes on (as Chaim Leb Kahan) to have 4 more children whose birth is recorded between 1874 and 1883, with a second wife, Freide Klein. Then his death was recorded in 1897. The record gives his age as 72, his parents' names as Joseph Kahan and Rifke née Berkovics. It also says he was born in Gyulafalu.

Here's the mystery: If Chaim Leb was born in Gyulafalu around 1825, how come there's no mention of him or his family until 1856, when he is almost 30? How did the family escape, for example, inclusion in the 1828 or 1848 censuses?

One possibility is that Chaim Leb was born elsewhere and emigrated to Gyulafalu a short time before 1856. Maybe, if the Hebrew Wikipedia is correct, he came from Sziget. In that case his death record would be just wrong about where he was born.

This is hard to rule out altogether but I don't think it's likely. The Jewish community in the area was small with few people coming in from any distance. Coming in from Sziget, especially from an important lineage, would be a salient fact about a person and not likely to be forgotten. Surely if the descendants know today they are related to the Kahana-Hellers, it would have been remembered by whoever registered Chaim Leb's death in 1897.

At this point it is worth commenting that while a trickle of Jews have arrived to the area in the late 18th and early 19th century, this rural, poor corner of the world was not an attractive immigration destination, as attested by the records showing few incomers. It is hard to imagine the circumstances that would bring the scion of a generally wealthy and educated urban family to settle in the area.

The explanation must lie elsewhere.

In 1882, a child named Abraham Moldawan Kahan died in Bréb, a village about 3 hours on foot from Gyulafalu. His parents were David Hers Moldawan & Reize; When Abraham was born, 2 years earlier, his father's name was registered as David Hers Kahan. David Hers was from Gyulafalu.

It seems that some people Gyulafalu and Bréb went both by Moldovan and Kahan. I'll return later to why this could be, but assuming it's true, things in fact start falling into place.

We find Chaim Leb's birth record, as Chaim, born in 1827 to a Joseph and Rifke Moldovan. He is then recorded, as Leb Moldovan born in Breb, in the 1848 census in Gyulafalu. He is 22 years old, married, and has a one year old son named Mojse. We also find a birth record of one more son of Chaim Leb and his second wife Freide, in 1867. This is a then a rarely complete set of life events from cradle to grave.

Other records seem to match. For example , Chaim Leb's son Mojse, as Moses Moldovan, has his first son Efrajem's birth recorded in 1869. We then find various records for Efrajem or Frojim Kahan which are consistent with this information.

Interestingly, a birth record for another of Moses's sons has his surname as "KATCZ / KAHAN". Katcz (Hungarian spelling for Katz) is another version of Kahan (see below) and it seems that again as late as 1886 they could be used somewhat interchangeably. And indeed some early 19th century census records Chaim's parents are named Katcz.

What is going on with those names then? Why are they being used interchangeably in the record?

There could be several explanations but I think the answer is simply that family names were new to Eastern Europe Jews in the 19th century, and probably, in these small remote villages, not in regular use outside of officialdom. This resulted in a level of inconsistency when it came to the records. This is by the way in spite of the requirement for fixed, German surnames for all Jews decreed by the Habsburg emperor as early as 1787.

Joseph Moldovan/Katcz must have come from Moldova (or possibly his family did in recent generations,) and so Moldovan was adopted as a "Toponymic" surname. He must have also been a Kohen, that is, believed to descent from biblical Aaron. This status would have been widely known as he would have undertaken some specific roles in religious rituals. People with this status often adopted surnames like Cohen, Kahan, Kahana or Katz.

In the 1830's the Joseph used Katcz and Moldovan interchangeably. He died in 1837 or 38, andit seems his family in Breb settled for Moldovan. However, one of his sons, Chaim Leb, married a woman from Gyulafalu and moved to that village. For some reason, in Gyulafalu they used the name Kahan, at first interchangeably with Moldovan. They switched permanently to Kahan towards the end of the century. One could speculate that there were other, unrelated, Moldovans in the village, and so switching to the equally appropriate Kahan helped to know who anyone is talking about at any given time.

If that's true, all the Kahans in the area are descendents of Chaim Leb, which is a useful fact when inferring the family tree.

This has caused, incidentally, extra confusion for Chaim's son, David Hersch, who married a Breb woman and moved back to Breb, where the family was not known as Kahan. This must have resulted in the "Kahan / Moldovan" record that put me first on the trail.

As a final remark, the original Wikipedia claims about the relationship to the Kahana-Hellers are unsourced. Obituaries for rabbi Chaim Arye Leb Kahana (1903-1956), the grandson of the original Chaim Leb Kahan discussed above, make no mention of this claim, and I haven't seen any other source directly from family members that references it.

Addendum - this question might be resolvable by genetic genealogy:

The Jewish-Maramaros Y-DNA project is working on establishing the paternal lines for Maramros Jewish families using Y-DNA. I understand from personal communication that they have in their sample a Molodovan from Breb, and so if any living Kahan/Kahana male descendant is to have their DNA tested, it would show whether in fact they are of the same paternal line. Read more about their results in their blog at https://jewsofmaramaros.com/.

Addendum 2:

  • As late as 1904, in the birth record of Avrum Leb Gancz (1904-), the mother's last name was recorded as Moldovan but the grandfather's last name was recorded as Kahan.
